# IPM and Crop Disease Knowledge Base

## Tomato Early Blight (Alternaria solani)
**Pathogen:** Alternaria solani (fungus)
**Conditions:** Favoured by warm temperatures (24–29°C), high humidity (>80%), and prolonged leaf wetness.

### Identification
- Dark brown/black lesions with concentric rings (target-board pattern) on older lower leaves
- Yellow halo around lesions
- Lesions coalesce; severe defoliation follows

### IPM Recommendations (Priority Order)
1. **Cultural (first line):** Remove and destroy infected leaf debris. Mulch base to reduce splash dispersal. Increase plant spacing for air flow. Avoid overhead irrigation; use drip.
2. **Biological:** Apply Trichoderma viride or Bacillus subtilis-based bio-fungicides. Spray 5% Neem Seed Kernel Extract (NSKE).
3. **Monitoring:** Scout weekly. Track humidity; spray before symptom spread if risk is high.
4. **Chemical Advisory (last resort):** Copper oxychloride, Mancozeb, or Azoxystrobin-based fungicides may be considered. Confirm with your local agriculture officer / Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) before applying any chemical treatment.

## Tomato Leaf Mold (Fulvia fulva)
**Pathogen:** Fulvia fulva (fungus)

## General IPM Principles
- **Scouting:** Regular (weekly) scouting is the foundation of IPM. Record pest and disease levels; act on Economic Threshold Levels (ETL), not prophylactically.
- **Cultural Controls:** Crop rotation, resistant varieties, sanitation, proper spacing, and avoiding overhead irrigation reduce disease pressure significantly.
- **Biological Controls:** Natural enemies, bio-fungicides (Trichoderma, Bacillus subtilis), and botanical extracts (neem) are the first choice after cultural methods.
- **Chemical Controls:** Only as last resort when threshold is exceeded. Always use least-toxic, targeted options. Rotate modes of action (FRAC/IRAC codes) to avoid resistance.

# Sustainable Agriculture Practices
## Integrated Pest Management (IPM)
Integrated Pest Management (IPM) is an effective and environmentally sensitive approach to pest management that relies on a combination of common-sense practices. IPM programs use current, comprehensive information on the life cycles of pests and their interaction with the environment. This information, in combination with available pest control methods, is used to manage pest damage by the most economical means, and with the least possible hazard to people, property, and the environment.

## Crop Rotation
Crop rotation is the practice of planting different crops sequentially on the same plot of land to improve soil health, optimize nutrients in the soil, and combat pest and weed pressure. For example, say a farmer has planted a field of corn. When the corn harvest is finished, he might plant beans, since corn consumes a lot of nitrogen and beans return nitrogen to the soil.

## Organic Practices
Organic agriculture is a production system that sustains the health of soils, ecosystems, and people. It relies on ecological processes, biodiversity and cycles adapted to local conditions, rather than the use of inputs with adverse effects.

## Irrigation Scheduling
Proper irrigation scheduling is critical for water conservation and crop health. Over-watering can lead to root rot and fungal diseases like late blight or powdery mildew. Under-watering stresses the plants, making them more susceptible to pests and reducing yield. Using soil moisture sensors and monitoring weather forecasts (rain probability) can help determine the optimal time to irrigate.

## Soil Health and Nutrient Management
Healthy soil is the foundation of sustainable agriculture. Adding organic matter, like compost or well-rotted manure, improves soil structure, water retention, and microbial activity. Nitrogen, Phosphorus, and Potassium (NPK) are the primary macronutrients needed by plants. However, excessive use of synthetic fertilizers can lead to nutrient runoff and environmental pollution.

## Disease Prevention
Preventing diseases is often more effective than treating them. Key practices include:
1. Planting disease-resistant varieties.
2. Ensuring adequate spacing between plants to improve air circulation and reduce humidity.
3. Watering at the base of the plant (drip irrigation) rather than overhead to keep leaves dry.
4. Promptly removing and destroying infected plant debris.

> **Note**: Confirm with your local agriculture officer / Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) before applying any chemical treatment.

Nitrogen (N) Deficiency: Characterized by general yellowing (chlorosis) of older lower leaves first, stunted vegetative growth, and thin stems. Remediation: Apply urea top-dressing (25-30 kg/ha) or neem-coated urea, supplemented with organic compost.

Phosphorus (P) Deficiency: Manifests as dark green to purplish anthocyanin pigmentation along leaf veins and petioles, delayed maturity, and poor root development. Remediation: Band application of Single Superphosphate (SSP) or Diammonium Phosphate (DAP) at root zone.

Calcium (Ca) Deficiency: Causes apical blossom-end rot in tomatoes, tip-burn in leafy greens, and distorted growing shoot tips due to poor cell wall pectate synthesis. Remediation: Foliar spray of calcium nitrate (0.5%) and maintaining uniform soil moisture.

Soil Moisture and Smart Irrigation: Sandy loam soils require frequent light irrigations (40-60% field capacity), while clay soils need deeper, less frequent watering to prevent root hypoxia. Drip irrigation saves 40-50% water and minimizes leaf wetness that triggers fungal pathogens.

Satellite NDVI Vegetation Monitoring: Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) is computed as (NIR - Red) / (NIR + Red). Values between 0.6 and 0.8 indicate vigorous green biomass. Drops below 0.35 signify severe drought stress, waterlogging, or pest infestation.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Principles: IPM combines cultural sanitation, physical barriers, biological antagonists (Trichoderma, Bacillus subtilis, Chrysoperla), economic threshold scouting, and judicious chemical application as a last resort to minimize environmental toxicity.

Weather and Plant Epidemiology: High relative humidity (>80%) combined with temperatures between 20-28°C accelerates conidial germination and zoospore mobility in blights. Wind gusts above 15 km/h disperse fungal spores across neighboring plots.
